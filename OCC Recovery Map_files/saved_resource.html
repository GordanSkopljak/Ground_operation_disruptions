OCC Recovery Map
Working draft · outside view

How an airline actually recovers from a disruption

A map of the decision flow in an operations control centre, drawn from public sources, marking the points where I think the decision still depends on something no system holds.

Gordan Skopljak v0.1 August 2026 Hamburg

Read this as a set of questions, not a set of findings. I have never worked in an OCC. Everything below is assembled from published research, regulation and vendor material, all listed at the end. I am circulating it at v0.1 because the parts I have wrong are more useful to me than the parts I have right, and the only people who can tell them apart have run the desk.

The sequence

Recovery is conventionally handled in a fixed order: aircraft first, then crew, then passengers. The research literature is direct about why this is a compromise. Sequential decisions miss the interdependencies between the three resources, so the answer is feasible rather than good, and controllers choose a workable option quickly rather than exploring alternatives.1 Integrated recovery, solving aircraft and crew jointly, is where the optimisation work is heading.

What interests me is not the optimisation gap. It is the second column of the picture: the inputs that are not in any system when the decision is made.

Stage by stage

01Detect and assess

Something breaks: a technical finding, weather, a crew member calling in sick, a flow slot moving. The event itself is visible to the system almost immediately. The judgement that follows is whether this is a contained delay or the first move in a cascade through the rotation, and that reading appears to come from experience rather than from data on the screen.

02Recover the aircraft

Controllers swap tails, reroute rotations, delay or cancel. The binding constraints here are the most machine readable of the whole sequence: tail availability, minimum turnaround, maintenance windows, airport slots, curfews, and the rule that all legs of a flight take the same aircraft or all cancel.1

The soft part is whether a constraint that looks fixed actually is. A book turnaround time and the time a particular handler at a particular outstation can achieve at 23:00 are not the same number, and I suspect the difference is carried by the controller who has dealt with that station before.

03Recover the crew

Now the aircraft plan meets flight time limitations, and this is where I find the clearest example of the gap. Under EASA rules a commander may extend the flight duty period at their discretion, by up to two hours for a two pilot crew and three for an augmented crew, always measured against the maximum daily FDP table and reduced by any extension already applied.2

So one of the most valuable options available during a recovery is legally real, quantifiable, and conditional on a specific person agreeing to it. A system can compute that the option exists. Whether it is available tonight, with this commander, after this kind of day, is a different question, and the crew controller who picks up the phone is the one holding the answer.

04Recover the passengers

Rebooking, accommodation, and compensation exposure under EU261. The counts are in the system. What seems not to be, at least not in a usable form, is the composition: which connections cascade into another airline, who is travelling as an unaccompanied minor, which passenger group makes a cancellation more expensive than the fare data suggests.

The claim I am least sure about

Delay codes under the IATA standard are assigned after the event, and they are the label attached to most historical operational data.3 A code records what the delay was attributed to. Attribution and physical cause are not obviously the same thing, particularly where the code determines which party absorbs the cost and is settled between an airline and a ground handler.

If that gap is wide, then a model trained on coded history is partly learning who was held responsible rather than what happened, and its recommendations would inherit that. If the gap is narrow, this concern is noise and I should drop it.

I cannot resolve this from outside. It is the single thing I would most like someone who codes delays to tell me.

What I expect to have wrong

  • The four stage split is probably too clean. Real recovery looks more like several loops than a pipeline, and I have drawn only one return path.
  • I have modelled a network carrier with a hub. Point to point and regional operations almost certainly break this shape.
  • I have said nothing about the handover between shifts, which several sources hint is where context is lost, and which I do not understand at all.
  • I have treated the OCC as one room. In practice crew control, maintenance control and network control may have genuinely competing objectives, and I have not drawn that tension anywhere.

Open questions

Each of these should take a couple of minutes to answer and none of them touch anything proprietary.

  1. What is the recommendation a system would produce that a controller would almost always override, and what is the reason behind the override?
  2. What information is reliably missing or late at the moment a recovery decision has to be made?
  3. Where does the OCC still fall back to the phone and a whiteboard rather than the system, and is that a tooling gap or a deliberate choice?
  4. How accurate are delay codes as a record of cause, in your experience, and does anyone treat them as training data?
  5. What does a station or ground crew know that never reaches the system in a usable form?
  6. When a recovery decision turns out badly, what is examined first: the decision, the information available at the time, or the person?
